Summary

Ripening is a continuation of development, not decay

Banana (Musa spp.) is one of the most widely eaten tropical fruits because of its nutritional value, taste and availability, yet it is also highly perishable. The fruit continues to ripen after harvest, and during that time physical and chemical changes alter its appearance, texture and taste.

This experiment followed six bananas from a single stem over seven days of storage. Peel colour, firmness and sweetness were scored daily against a five-point scale. The peel moved from uniform dark green on Day 1 to full yellow by Days 5–6, and brown senescent spotting had begun by Day 7. The fruit kept its structural integrity until Day 3 and then softened rapidly between Days 4 and 7, ending soft and mushy. Sweetness rose from astringent and starchy on Day 1, through the first detectable sweetness on Day 4, to a rich sugary profile on Day 7.

Together these three trends confirm the climacteric pattern: a lag phase in which internal ethylene stays basal, followed by an autocatalytic ethylene rise that drives pigment breakdown, starch hydrolysis and cell-wall disassembly at the same time.
