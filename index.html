Ripening of the Cavendish banana from mature-green to edible-ripe

Abstract

Ripening is an ethylene-driven programme, not decay

Bananas are harvested at the mature-green stage and ripen after harvest because they are climacteric: a small autocatalytic burst of ethylene triggers a coordinated sequence of physiological changes — chlorophyll degradation, starch hydrolysis, cell-wall disassembly and volatile synthesis — that convert a hard, starchy, astringent fruit into a soft, sweet, aromatic one. This portfolio documents that sequence as it occurs in five Cavendish fingers held at ambient room temperature.

The fingers were scored daily for peel colour, firmness and sweetness on a single five-point scale and photographed under identical lighting. Peel colour advanced from 1 to 5 over seven days, holding for two days before the autocatalytic phase and reaching brown spotting by Day 7. The firmness score rose from 1 to 5 — the fruit went from very hard to mushy — and sweetness rose from 1 to 5 between the first and third destructive samples, from astringent and starchy to very sweet.

The results are consistent with the accepted model of climacteric ripening and illustrate why maturity indices at harvest, temperature management and ethylene control are the three levers available to anyone handling this crop after harvest.
