Objective, materials & methods

What the experiment set out to show, what was used, how it was carried out, and exactly how each observation was scored — so that the results can be repeated by anyone.

2.1 · Objective

Objective of the experiment

To document and explain the ripening of five Cavendish banana fingers (Musa acuminata, AAA) from the mature-green stage until the fruit becomes overripe and leaves the edible window, by:

  1. Observing daily for seven days

    Recording peel colour, firmness and sweetness at roughly the same time each day, and photographing the fruit at every stage.

  2. Applying one fixed five-point scoring scale

    Scoring peel colour, firmness and taste from 1 to 5 against the definitions in Section 2.4, which were agreed and printed before the first observation.

  3. Sampling internally at three stages

    Cutting a different finger for each internal observation — early, intermediate and overripe — so that the same fruit was never cut twice.

  4. Explaining the observed changes

    Linking each trend to the underlying ethylene-driven physiology, supported by published literature, and discussing the limitations of the design.

2.3 · Procedure

How the experiment was carried out

  1. Select and label the fruit

    Five mature-green Cavendish fingers were taken from one bunch on the day of harvest and labelled a to e with a small paper label on the pedicel, never on the peel. Fingers a and b were designated for daily observation; c, d and e were reserved for destructive sampling.

  2. Record the baseline (Day 1)

    Peel colour, firmness and taste were scored for fingers a, b and c. Finger c was then cut as the early internal sample and its pulp described.

  3. Set up the ripening environment

    The fingers were laid in a single layer on a ventilated tray at ambient room temperature, away from direct sunlight and away from other produce. No ethylene was added and no ethylene absorbent was used, so ripening was driven entirely by the fruit's own ethylene.

  4. Observe daily at the same time

    Each day, for seven consecutive days, fingers a and b were scored for peel colour (1–5) and firmness (1–5). Sweetness (1–5) could only be judged by tasting, so it was recorded only on the days a finger was cut.

  5. Photograph under identical conditions

    The fingers were placed on the same neutral background at the same distance and camera angle under the same light source. Images were labelled with the day and ripening stage immediately after capture.

  6. Sample destructively at three stages

    A separate finger was cut on Day 1 (finger c, early), Day 4 (finger d, intermediate) and Day 7 (finger e, overripe). Each was photographed whole and in cross-section and the pulp described. No finger was cut twice, so a and b ripened undisturbed from day 1 to day 7.

  7. Continue to the overripe stage

    Observation was continued until brown spots appeared on the peel, because the end of the edible window is itself a postharvest quality issue.

  8. Tabulate and plot the data

    Scores were entered into a single table (Table 4) and plotted against day (Figures 13–14) so that the direction and rate of each change could be compared directly.

2.4 · Scoring scales

One scale, defined before scoring began

Subjective observations are only useful if the scale is fixed in advance and applied identically every day. All three attributes were scored 1–5 against the definitions below, printed as a reference card before Day 1.

Peel-colour scale (1–5)

Scored on the sun-exposed face of the middle fingers, using the proportion of yellow on the peel as the criterion.

Table 3 · Scoring scale reference table
Score Peel colour (visual) Firmness (physical touch) Taste / sweetness (flavour)
1 All green. Entirely green and hard, no yellow present. Very hard. Very firm and completely hard. Astringent / starchy. Puckering in the mouth and inedible.
2 Green with a trace of yellow. Slightly yellow at the edges but mostly green. Hard. Slightly yielding but still quite firm. Slightly sour. Not very sweet, but still firm in texture and mild in taste.
3 More yellow than green. Progressing fruit; edges still green. Moderately firm. Moderately yielding to a light squeeze. Slightly sweet. Reduced starch and a good taste.
4 All yellow. Peel entirely yellow; the fruit is ripe. Soft / edible. Relatively soft and gives somewhat to a light squeeze. Sweet and flavourful. Has banana taste and fragrance.
5 Yellow with brown spots. Overripe; peel yellow with some brown spots. Very soft / mushy. Mushy, spongy or oversoft. Very sweet / overripe. Very soft and very sweet.

Why firmness counts upward as the fruit softens

Firmness is scored as a softness rank, not a measured force: 1 is the hardest fruit and 5 is the mushiest. That means an increasing firmness score on Figure 14 indicates softening, and the two curves for firmness and sweetness rise together. If you would rather plot the physical trend downward, invert the scale in data.js and update the axis label.

Why taste is missing on some days

Sweetness can only be judged by eating the fruit, and the fruit cannot be eaten and kept. Taste was therefore recorded only on days 1, 4 and 7 — the three days a finger was cut — while colour and firmness were scored every day on fingers a and b.

Method note — destructive sampling

Internal colour and taste can only be assessed by cutting the fruit, so a single fruit cannot be followed internally from green to ripe. Following the assignment brief, three separate fingers were sacrificed at the early, intermediate and overripe stages — c on Day 1, d on Day 4 and e on Day 7. The two intact fingers give the continuous daily record; the three cut fingers give the internal evidence.
