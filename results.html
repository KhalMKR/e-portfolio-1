4 · Results

Seven days of storage

The evaluation of the postharvest ripening of the banana samples was conducted over a seven-day storage period. Peel colour, firmness and sweetness were monitored using a standardised scoring scale from 1 to 5.

Table 1 · Scoring scale reference table
Score Peel colour (visual) Firmness (physical touch) Taste / sweetness (flavour)
1 All green. Entirely green and hard with no yellow present. Very hard. Very firm and completely hard. Astringent / starchy. Puckering in the mouth and inedible.
2 Green with yellow trace. Slightly yellow at the edges but mostly green. Hard. Slightly yielding but still quite firm. Slightly sour. Not very sweet but still firm in texture and has a mild taste.
3 More yellow than green. Progressing fruit; edges are still green. Moderately firm. Moderately yielding to a light squeeze. Slightly sweet. Reduced starch and a good taste.
4 All yellow. Peels are all yellow and the fruit is ripe. Soft / edible. Relatively soft and gives somewhat to a light squeeze. Sweet and flavourful. Has banana taste and a fragrance.
5 Yellow with brown spots. Overripe; peels are yellow with some brown spots. Very soft / mushy. Mushy, spongy or oversoft. Very sweet / overripe. Very soft and very sweet.

4.3 · Visual and physical trends

What the three parameters showed

The bananas showed clear changes from uniform dark green on Day 1 to full yellow by Days 5–6, and by Day 7 brown senescent spotting had begun.

The firmness of the banana showed a direct relationship to ripening time. The fruit maintained its structural integrity until Day 3 before undergoing rapid softening between Days 4 and 7, ending with a soft and mushy texture.

The flavour and sweetness of the banana increased throughout the observation period. Astringency and starchiness dominated the early stages, sweetness appeared by Day 4, and the profile peaked into a rich, sugary taste by Day 7.

Read together, the three parameters did not move in lockstep. Colour ran ahead of firmness — the peel was fully yellow on Day 5 while the fruit was still only moderately soft — and sweetness could only be sampled on three of the seven days. 5 · Discussion

Why the fruit changed

5.1 · Physiological classification and the respiratory climacteric

The observed ripening stages confirm the classification of banana as a climacteric fruit. Unlike non-climacteric crops, climacteric fruits exhibit a surge in respiration rate together with an increase in ethylene biosynthesis. Ethylene acts as a phytohormone regulator which initiates and coordinates the ripening of the fruit (Tipu & Sherif, 2024).

In this study, the lag phase observed between Days 1 and 2 is similar to pre-climacteric dormancy, where internal ethylene remained basal. The transition from Day 3 onward marked the start of the climacteric rise, where autocatalytic ethylene production stimulated rapid metabolic shifts across the pigment, carbohydrate and cell-wall fractions. The unchanged scores on Day 3 are consistent with that lag: the fruit had begun to turn but had not yet entered the rapid phase.

5.2 · Pigment degradation and colour evolution

The transition of peel colour from green to yellow and subsequently brown is driven by two distinct biochemical mechanisms. First, chlorophyll breakdown: in green mature fruits, chlorophyll masks the accessory pigments. Ripening triggers chlorophyllase and other catabolic enzymes that degrade the porphyrin ring and phytol tail of chlorophyll a and b. This degradation unmasks pre-existing carotenoids, shifting the visual phenotype to a vibrant yellow.

Second, once the fruit passes edible ripeness, senescence follows: cell membranes lose integrity, phenolic compounds oxidise and brown spotting appears on the peel — the score-5 stage recorded on Day 7.

5.3 · Carbohydrate metabolism and flavour development

The shift from an astringent, starchy profile to a sweet, flavourful condition is driven by several factors. During the green stage, carbon is stored primarily as amyloplast starch. Upon ethylene induction, hydrolytic enzymes accelerate the conversion of insoluble polymeric starch into soluble sugars. This osmotic shift increases sugar concentration, removing starch astringency and producing the characteristic sweet taste and the volatile ester aromas associated with edible ripeness.

Because sweetness was only tasted on Days 1, 4 and 7, the trend is a three-point series rather than a continuous curve. It nonetheless brackets the whole range: starchy and inedible, then slightly sweet, then very sweet.

5.4 · Textural softening and cell-wall modification

The continuous decline of firmness results from the enzymatic disassembly of the plant cell-wall matrix and middle lamella. Pectic substances, hemicellulose and cellulose undergo solubilisation and depolymerisation mediated by enzymes such as polygalacturonase, pectin methylesterase and β-cellulase. As intercellular adhesion decreases and cell turgor pressure changes, tissue cohesion breaks down — accounting for the increased compressibility and eventual mushiness observed on the final storage day.

This explains the shape of the firmness curve in Figure 10. The early plateau (Days 1–3) reflects a wall that is still largely intact; the steep section from Day 4 corresponds to the point at which the hydrolytic enzymes have accumulated enough product to separate cells from one another.

5.5 · Limitations

  • One stem, six samples. All samples came from a single bunch, so there is no independent replication and the results describe this fruit rather than bananas in general.
  • Subjective scoring. Colour, firmness and taste were judged by eye, by hand and by mouth. A colour chart, a penetrometer or a refractometer would make the scores reproducible by another group.
  • Five levels is coarse. Two consecutive days can carry the same score even when the fruit changed visibly (Days 2–3, Days 5–6), so a difference of one point is not necessarily meaningful.
  • Sweetness sampled on three days only. Tasting requires cutting the fruit, so the sweetness line in Figure 10 is an interpolation between three points.
  • Temperature and humidity were not recorded. Ripening rate is strongly temperature-dependent, so some of the day-to-day variation may be environmental rather than physiological.
