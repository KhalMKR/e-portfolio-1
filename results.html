3.4 · Discussion

How and why the fruit changed

Peel colour: chlorophyll goes, carotenoids stay

The first visible change appeared on Day 2, when the edges of the fingers turned slightly yellow while the rest of the peel remained green. From there the score climbed one step every one to two days until brown spotting appeared on Day 7. The yellow is not newly made pigment: banana peel already contains carotenoids, which are masked by chlorophyll. Ethylene induces chlorophyll degradation through chlorophyllase and pheophytinase, exposing the yellow and orange carotenoids underneath (Seymour et al., 2013).

The two-day plateau on Days 2 and 3 is the most informative feature of Figure 13. It is the point at which ethylene concentration is still too low to trigger the fruit's own synthesis; once autocatalytic (System 2) production begins, colour change accelerates through Days 4 to 6 and then stops climbing only because the chlorophyll has run out and the next visible stage is browning (Bapat et al., 2010).

Firmness: the cell wall is taken apart

Firmness moved from 1 (very hard) to 5 (mushy) over the same seven days, but on a different schedule from colour. Softening is not one event but the cumulative action of several enzyme families — polygalacturonase and pectate lyase solubilise the middle lamella, pectin methylesterase alters the degree of pectin methylation, β-galactosidase strips side chains, and expansins loosen the wall non-enzymatically (Giovannoni, 2004). Because these enzymes act on different substrates at different rates, firmness keeps falling after the peel has already reached full yellow.

That lag is visible in Figure 14: firmness held at 3 on Day 5, when the peel was already fully yellow, and only then climbed to 4 and 5 on Days 6 and 7. The practical consequence is that colour is a poor guide on its own — fruit can look ready while still being firm enough to handle, and conversely can be soft while still slightly green.

Sweetness: starch becomes sugar

Sweetness rose from 1 to 3 by Day 4 and to 5 by Day 7. In the mature-green fruit most carbohydrate is stored as starch, which is tasteless and chalky; ripening activates α-amylase and starch phosphorylase to release glucose and maltose, and sucrose-phosphate synthase re-assembles much of it as sucrose (Wills & Golding, 2016). This is why the Day 1 sample tasted astringent rather than merely bland: the sugar was physically present, but locked in an insoluble polymer.

The jump from 1 to 3 between Day 1 and Day 4 is the largest single change in the dataset. It coincides with the start of the autocatalytic phase and with the first slight softening, which fits the accepted model in which starch hydrolysis and cell-wall disassembly are both ethylene-induced and roughly simultaneous. Full flavour arrived later still: the volatile esters that give banana its aroma — isoamyl acetate and butyl acetate — are formed by alcohol dehydrogenase and alcohol acyltransferase from fatty acid and amino acid precursors, and aroma is typically the last attribute to develop (Saltveit, 1999).

Reading the two curves together

Figure 14 was built to test one simple prediction: if softening and sweetening are both outputs of the same ethylene programme, their scores should rise together. They do — both lines start at 1, both are mid-scale by Day 4 and both reach 5 by Day 7 — but they do not rise at the same rate. Softening is continuous and gradual, whereas sweetness is a series of steps between the days on which a finger was cut. Part of that difference is biology and part is the design: sweetness was sampled on only three days, so its line is a straight interpolation and cannot show the small day-to-day variation that firmness does.

The internal samples and the end of the window

The three cut fingers confirm the same sequence from the inside. Finger c (Day 1) was hard, pale and starchy with an astringent, inedible taste. Finger d (Day 4) had begun to soften, the starch taste had dropped and sweetness was detectable. Finger e (Day 7) was very soft and mushy and very sweet — the fruit had passed its best eating quality, and the brown spots on the peel marked the same change.

That last point is worth stating plainly: by Day 7 all three attributes scored 5, and on this scale 5 means both “very sweet” and “overripe”. The experiment therefore cannot separate the peak of eating quality from the beginning of decay, because the scale does not have a distinct point for each.

Unexpected results and limitations

  • Sweetness looks like it collapses in the first chart. The original spreadsheet graph plotted “–” (not assessed) as 0, which drew the sweetness line down to zero on Days 2, 3, 5 and 6. Figure 14 here treats those days as missing values and joins only the days that were actually scored, which is why the two graphs look different. The correction does not change the underlying data.
  • The scale cannot distinguish “ripe” from “overripe” at the top end. Score 5 is defined as very sweet and overripe, so Day 7 scores full marks in every category even though the fruit was already past its best. A six-point scale, or a separate “overripe” flag, would separate the two.
  • Five levels is coarse. Two consecutive days can carry the same score even when the fruit changed noticeably (Days 2 and 3; Days 5 and 6). Differences of one point are therefore not necessarily meaningful.
  • Each internal sample was a different finger. Fingers c, d and e may have differed slightly in maturity before the experiment began, so part of the difference between the three internal descriptions reflects the fruit rather than time.
  • No instrumental measurement. Colour, firmness and taste were judged by eye, by hand and by mouth. A colorimeter, a penetrometer or a refractometer would have made the scores reproducible by another group.
  • Temperature was not logged. Ripening rate is strongly temperature-dependent, so day-to-day variation in how fast the scores moved may be environmental rather than physiological.
  • No ethylene measurement and no replication. The autocatalytic burst was inferred from the shape of the curves, not measured, and all five fingers came from one bunch. The findings describe this fruit under these conditions, not Cavendish bananas in general.
