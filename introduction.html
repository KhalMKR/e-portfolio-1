Introduction

Why banana is a perishable fruit, why it is classed as climacteric, and how its ripening can be read from the peel, the touch and the taste.

1.1 · The selected fruit

Banana — Musa spp.

Banana (Musa spp.) is one of the most widely eaten tropical fruits because of its nutritional value, taste and availability. Yet bananas are highly perishable fruits that continue to undergo ripening after harvesting. During this process, physical and chemical changes occur which affect the appearance, texture and taste of the fruit.

The ripening process can be observed from three changes: peel colour, firmness and sweetness. The green peel gradually turns yellow as chlorophyll breaks down. At the same time the firmness of the banana decreases, causing it to become softer. The sweetness also increases, because stored starch is converted into simple sugars, producing the characteristic sweet taste of a ripe banana.

Because these changes continue after the fruit has been detached from the plant, the condition of a banana at any moment is the product of its handling after harvest — not only of how mature it was when it was cut.

1.2 · Ripening physiology

A climacteric fruit

Bananas are classified as climacteric fruits because they continue to ripen after harvesting through changes in respiration and ethylene production. These processes play an important role in controlling the ripening stages and the overall quality of the fruit.

Unlike non-climacteric crops, climacteric fruits exhibit a surge in respiration rate together with an increase in ethylene biosynthesis. Ethylene acts as a phytohormone regulator which initiates and coordinates the ripening of the fruit (Tipu & Sherif, 2024). Once the fruit begins to produce its own ethylene, the production becomes autocatalytic: the gas stimulates further synthesis in the tissue around it, so ripening, once started, runs to completion even without any external source.

That behaviour has a direct practical consequence. Because ethylene is a gas, a single ripening banana accelerates the ripening of every fruit stored with it, which is why ventilation and the separation of ripe and unripe stock matter in handling.

1.3 · The ripening hormone

Three functions of ethylene, one per parameter

Each of the three parameters scored in this experiment is the visible output of a different ethylene-induced process.

Function 01 · colour

Pigment degradation

Ethylene triggers chlorophyllase and other catabolic enzymes that degrade the porphyrin ring and phytol tail of chlorophyll a and b. The breakdown unmasks carotenoids that were already present, shifting the peel from green to yellow.

Function 02 · sweetness

Starch hydrolysis

In the green stage, carbon is stored as amyloplast starch. Ethylene induction accelerates the conversion of insoluble polymeric starch into soluble sugars, removing the starchy astringency and producing the sweet taste of ripe fruit.

Function 03 · firmness

Cell-wall disassembly

Pectic substances, hemicellulose and cellulose are solubilised and depolymerised by enzymes such as polygalacturonase, pectin methylesterase and β-cellulase, so intercellular adhesion falls and the tissue loses its resistance to compression.

Why this matters for the experiment

If all three changes are outputs of one hormone-driven programme, they should move at roughly the same time — but not necessarily at the same rate. The experiment tests exactly that: whether colour, firmness and sweetness rise together over the seven days of storage.

1.4 · Judging maturity

Maturity indices for banana

A maturity index is a measurable characteristic that separates fruit ready to be harvested from fruit that is not. “Mature” is not the same as “ripe”: a mature fruit has the capacity to ripen properly; a ripe one has already done so.

Why peel colour is the index used here

Colour is non-destructive, cheap, repeatable and strongly correlated with internal ripeness in banana. Its weakness is subjectivity, which is why the scale must be written down and applied under the same lighting every day — the approach taken in this experiment.

What the scale does not capture

Colour says nothing about sugar content, and it can mislead after cold storage or gas treatment. That is why two further parameters — firmness by touch and sweetness by taste — were scored alongside it rather than relying on appearance alone.
