Introduction

The fruit, why it ripens after harvest, the role of ethylene, and the indices used to decide when it is mature enough to harvest.

1.1 · The selected fruit

Cavendish banana — Musa acuminata AAA genome group

The banana is the fruit of a large herbaceous perennial in the genus Musa. The commercial dessert banana — the Cavendish type used in this study — is a triploid (AAA) parthenocarpic clone of M. acuminata, which is why the fruit develops without fertilisation and therefore contains no viable seeds. Because the crop is clonally propagated, every finger in a consignment tends to behave in a very similar way, which makes banana an unusually clean subject for a ripening experiment.

Banana and plantain together are among the most important food commodities traded globally, with annual world production exceeding 130 million tonnes (FAOSTAT, 2023). A large share of that crop is harvested while still hard and green and is expected to ripen during transport and retail — a practice that only works because banana is climacteric and continues to ripen after detachment from the plant.

Postharvest losses in the crop are substantial. Roughly 20–40 % of production is lost between harvest and consumption in tropical producing regions, most of it from mechanical damage, premature ripening and chilling injury during handling (Kader, 2002; Wills & Golding, 2016). Understanding the ripening sequence is therefore not a purely academic exercise: it determines when to harvest, how cold to store, and when to intervene with ethylene or an ethylene antagonist such as 1-MCP.

Why this fruit was chosen

  • Clearly climacteric. Ripening is dramatic, reproducible and visible to the naked eye, so daily observation yields a legible trend rather than noise.
  • Well-defined maturity indices. Peel colour, finger fullness and pulp-to-peel ratio are all documented, so our scoring scale can be tied to published standards.
  • Available at a consistent maturity. Five fingers taken from one bunch give a group of practically identical physiological age.
  • Destructive sampling is practical. Individual fingers can be removed from the hand without disturbing the remainder.

1.2 · Ripening physiology

What “climacteric” actually means

Fruits are divided into two groups by their postharvest respiratory behaviour. Non-climacteric fruits (citrus, grape, strawberry, pineapple) ripen only while attached to the plant: once detached, their respiration declines steadily and they never improve in eating quality. Climacteric fruits (banana, mango, papaya, tomato, avocado) show a characteristic rise in respiration — the climacteric — that coincides with a burst of ethylene production and with the transformation of the fruit into its ripe state.

The distinction matters practically. A non-climacteric fruit must be harvested ripe; a climacteric fruit can be harvested hard and green, shipped, and then ripened on demand. The climacteric rise is also autocatalytic: the ethylene produced by the fruit stimulates further ethylene production, so once ripening starts it proceeds to completion even if the external ethylene source is removed.

Two ethylene systems are recognised. System 1 operates at very low levels in unripe fruit and in vegetative tissue; at the onset of ripening it is superseded by System 2, the autocatalytic burst that drives the ripening programme (Bapat et al., 2010; Seymour et al., 2013). Our experiment relies on System 2: fruit was held without any added ethylene, so the burst we observed was generated by the fruit itself.

1.3 · The ripening hormone

Functions of ethylene in ripening

Ethylene (C2H4) is a gaseous plant hormone active at trace concentrations — a few tenths of a microlitre per litre is enough to trigger ripening.

How the fruit makes it

methionine → S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) → 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) → ethylene

The two committed steps are catalysed by ACC synthase (ACS) and ACC oxidase (ACO). Both are encoded by multigene families whose expression is strongly induced at the onset of ripening — which is exactly what makes the burst autocatalytic. Ethylene is then perceived by membrane receptors such as ETR1, and the signal is relayed through CTR1 and EIN2/EIN3-like transcription factors to the promoters of ripening-related genes (Giovannoni, 2004).

Function 01

Colour change

Ethylene induces chlorophyll degradation (chlorophyllase, pheophytinase) and the unmasking/stabilisation of carotenoids, so the peel turns from green to yellow. This is the most visible marker of ripening and the basis of our colour index.

Function 02

Softening

Cell-wall hydrolases — polygalacturonase, pectate lyase, pectin methylesterase, β-galactosidase — plus expansins dissolve the middle lamella and loosen the wall, dropping firmness from “hard” to “yielding” within days.

Function 03

Starch → sugar

Starch is hydrolysed by α-amylase and starch phosphorylase and re-synthesised into sucrose by sucrose-phosphate synthase. °Brix rises from roughly 4 to 20, and the starchy taste is replaced by sweetness.

Function 04

Aroma formation

Fatty acids and amino acids are converted to volatile esters (isoamyl acetate, butyl acetate) by alcohol dehydrogenase and alcohol acyltransferase. Aroma is the last quality attribute to develop and a sensitive indicator of overripeness.

Function 05

Acidity & astringency

Organic acids (malate, citrate) are respired away and tannins polymerise into insoluble forms, so the fruit becomes less tart and loses its mouth-puckering astringency.

Function 06

Respiration & senescence

Ethylene raises the respiration rate to supply the ATP and carbon skeletons ripening demands. Past the ripe stage the same hormone accelerates senescence, which is why overripe fruit deteriorates quickly.

Relevance to handling

Because ethylene is a gas, it spreads. A single ripe fruit in a box of green ones will ripen the whole box; a rotting fruit will do the same, faster. Ventilation, segregation of ripe and unripe stock, and ethylene scrubbing (or 1-MCP treatment to block the receptor) are all direct applications of the physiology summarised above.

1.4 · Deciding when to harvest

Maturity indices for banana

A maturity index is a measurable characteristic that reliably separates a fruit that is ready to be harvested from one that is not. “Mature” is not the same as “ripe”: a mature fruit has the capacity to ripen properly, a ripe one has already done so.

Table 1 · Maturity indices used commercially for banana
Index What is measured Target at harvest Use in this study
Peel colour Visual colour stage of the peel, scored against a chart Index 1–2 (green, at most a trace of yellow) Primary index — scored daily
Finger fullness Loss of longitudinal angularity as the finger fills out 75–80 % full grade (“three-quarter full”) Used when selecting fruit
Pulp : peel ratio Mass of pulp relative to peel ≈ 1.2–1.4 Destructive check at sampling
Finger diameter Calliper measurement at the mid-point Cultivar-specific minimum Used when selecting fruit
Dry matter Oven-dry mass as a percentage of fresh mass ≈ 22–25 % Not measured (optional)
Age / heat units Days or accumulated degree-days from bunch emergence ≈ 90–120 days Recorded from the supplier
Latex colour Colour of exudate at the cut pedicel Milky white (not watery) Observed during destructive sampling

Why colour is used as the primary index

Colour is non-destructive, cheap, repeatable and strongly correlated with internal ripeness in banana. A trained scorer can place a finger on a five-point scale within seconds. Its weakness is subjectivity, which is why the scale must be defined explicitly and applied under constant lighting — the approach adopted here and described in Section 2.4.

Why objective indices still matter

Colour can be misleading after cold storage or in fruit that has been gassed, and it says nothing about sugar content. Commercial operations therefore pair the visual index with at least one objective measure — dry matter, pulp : peel ratio or °Brix. Our experiment relied on the visual index alone; adding one objective measure is the single change that would most improve a repeat of it.
